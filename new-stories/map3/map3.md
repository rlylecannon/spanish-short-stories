Two Afro-Latin and two non-Afro stories were replaced, so the Afro-Latin share rises from 10 to 12 of 20 and each level keeps 4 non-Afro stories.

**Placement.** Barlovento (drums and cacao) went to C1, where the remaining Afro-Latin stories had little music once the marimba story left, while C2 already has candombe, saya and panalivio. Veracruz went to C2 so that the two Afro-Mexican stories (Costa Chica, Veracruz) sit at different levels and on different coasts. The Dominican kitchen story takes C2's former food slot. Esmeraldas takes C1's former food slot as a livelihood and place story.

**Esmeraldas: which tradition.** The marimba is the best documented (UNESCO 2015, jointly with Colombia), but the Colombian marimba story was replaced because of that overlap. The concheras of the mangroves and the oral décima esmeraldeña are also well documented and give a story with a different engine (science against a middleman, with an elder who is right), so the marimba stays in the background. The bomba del Chota belongs to Imbabura and Carchi, not Esmeraldas, and was not used. Chigualos (wakes for children) were left out because c2-08 already turns on a death.

**Cuba or the Dominican Republic.** The Dominican Republic fits better. Havana is already an A1–B2 setting, and Cuba's best-documented Afro traditions (rumba, tumba francesa) would make a fourth drum-and-song plot at C2. San Pedro de Macorís's cocolo heritage (guloyas, yaniqueque, baseball) gives a present-day kitchen story with English contact, which keeps the code-switching topic the Limón story carried and does not repeat c1-09's 1870s archive and ledgers. The Congos of Villa Mella were considered, but a funeral confraternity of Congo origin would echo Palenque (c2-08).

**Grammar re-balance.** C1 lost pps, conc and futp (c1-03) and conc, deahi, serid and regsh (c1-04), all shared, but concessives were held only by these two stories. The new C1 stories take haya + participle, the future perfect, venir/andar + gerund and the compound infinitive (c1-03), and the concessives, de ahí que, register shifts and idiomatic ser/estar (c1-04). Every C1 topic is now in 2 stories except the concessives (1). C2 lost rumour, contact and literary -ra (c2-06) and conjecture, por poco, irony and colloquial subjunctive formulas (c2-03). The new C2 stories take contact, colloquial formulas, por poco and irony (c2-03), and rumour, literary -ra, conjecture and proverbs (c2-06), which lifts proverbs from 1 story to 2. All 20 C2 topics are now in exactly 2 stories, and no B2 headline is reused.

**Word targets.** C1 is unchanged (1,100–1,600). The C2 targets were re-spaced to 1,500–1,700 … 1,700–1,900, so none is above 1,900.

**Can-do file.** All 20 statements per level are kept with the same ids. The examples or region lists in C1-R4, C1-L1, C2-R4 and C2-L1 were updated to name the new stories' traditions and varieties. Evidence lists were updated for C1-R4, L1 (unchanged ids), L3 and S3, and for C2-R4, L1, S2, S4, W3, W4 and G2. The other 16 stories are unchanged except for their C2 word targets and the updated can-do wording they quote.

- **c1-01 El manifiesto de Almirante**: Yarisel Archbold, 34, a cargo dispatcher at the Almirante dock and granddaughter of Afro-Antillean banana workers, finds that two cold boxes of insulin and vaccines for the hospital on Isla Colón appear on the ferry manifest but never reached the island, and the port authority wants a name for its incident report by Monday. Half the town says Yonaiker Ascanio, 19, a culo e' puya drummer seen loading sacks onto a pickup at dawn, has sold them on, and her great-uncle Críspulo Mijares, 79, the cooperative's oldest fermenter, only says that the cacao 'is where it has to be'. Mariángel discovers that Críspulo had asked Yonaiker to carry the best fermented beans to his house to roast them for the chocolate his late wife served the drummers every San Juan, a family promise kept since 1980; she renegotiates the shipment by email, makes up the weight from the next fermentation, and drinks the chocolate at the velorio of 23 June.
- **c1-04 Cuatro centímetros y medio**: Wilmer Quiñónez, 27, a biologist from Esmeraldas city who measures black cockles (concha prieta) every month with the concheras of San Lorenzo, must tell the provincial office by Friday whether one estero should be closed, because his data show shells shrinking below the legal 4.5 centimetres. At the same time a middleman, Leonidas Cabezas, offers the women's association a contract for every cockle they can dig before the closed season, 'measured or not', and the association's leader, Ermelinda Corozo, 64, a conchera and decimera, refuses both the contract and the closure and says the estero is sick, not overharvested. Wilmer finds she is right: a shrimp farm upstream has cut the mangrove and silted the channel, so the cockles grow slowly there and not because the women take small ones; his report recommends rotating esteros instead of a blanket closure, the association turns down the middleman, and Ermelinda recites a décima about the mud at the assembly.
- **c1-05 La voz de Triunfo**: Dionisio Arzú, 41, producer at a small Garífuna community radio station, is preparing the 12 April special when a hotel developer offers to pay for a new transmitter on one condition: the broadcast must not mention the 2015 Inter-American Court ruling on the community's land. With 17-year-old intern Keyla Suazo, Dionisio traces the voice to his own great-aunt Paulina Guity, whose song is about the land itself; he turns down the sponsor, airs the song with the ruling, and the community pays for the transmitter through a radio-thon.

The forecast is bad, the insurance rules are worse, and his grandson Yerko suspects the old man only wants to move the house to sell it. Javiera discovers that the date matches the year's highest tide, the only one that lets the house float over the reef to the plot where his late wife was born; she approves the minga with conditions, one tide later, and the whole island pulls.

- **c1-09 El cocinero de la goleta**: Ezequiel Rivera Ortiz, 47, an archivist in San Juan, receives a request from Altagracia Peguero, 38, a Dominican lawyer tracing an ancestor named Tiburcio who appears in Puerto Rico's 1872 slave register. Tracing a technical word in the verse that only an insider would know, Simona finds that Jacinta did write it, with help from the site's own drainage foreman, and that the copla warns, correctly, that the planned drain will flood the old cemetery; Simona redesigns the drainage, and the next copla praises her, with a sting in the tail.
- **c2-05 Demasiado perfecta**: Neftalí Chicas, 64, custodian at Joya de Cerén for thirty years, sees an online post offering an intact Classic-period pot 'from Cerén' to foreign collectors, and the next morning a national paper reports that the site has probably been looted, on his watch. Field memory and academic method together show it is a replica made for the site's own education programme and stolen from a storeroom; the report corrects the press, and the paper corrects itself, on page 14.

Ruperto knows the fandango sings the prince, but the colonial record he trusts speaks of a maroon leader, a Spanish campaign in 1609 and a town of free Black people recognised years later, and his old master, the versador don Fidencio Cházaro, 84, tells him that a town that needs a prince has not understood its own story. Ruperto rewrites the plaque so that the bronze records only what the documents say and leaves the legend to the décimas, and on the anniversary night don Fidencio sings both versions on the tarima, the legend and the record, to a square that applauds them equally.
- **c2-07 Ndaje**: Perla Benítez, 47, a sworn Guaraní–Spanish court interpreter in Asunción, is called in when the trial of Derlis Cañete, accused of setting fire to a neighbour's cane field, hinges on an elderly witness who speaks only Guaraní.
